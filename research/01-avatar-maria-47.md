## 🚩 Key Challenges & Pain Points

### 1. Pores and texture — the thing she sees first

- Her pores, especially on the nose and cheeks, look larger every year and she cannot make them
  smaller no matter what she buys. **[E — the page's own IG comment and lead testimonial are
  both pore-first]**
- Foundation stops looking smooth after a few hours; it settles into the pores and into the
  lines around her mouth, so makeup makes the problem *more* visible by lunchtime.
- Her skin looks fine in the mirror and bad in photos — particularly the front camera, and
  particularly in the flat overhead light at work.

### 2. Losing firmness and looking tired — the thing that actually hurts

- The jawline and cheeks have softened. Nothing has "fallen," but the face she sees isn't the
  one she has in her head.
- Fine lines on the forehead, between the brows and around the mouth now stay visible when her
  face is at rest, not just when she smiles.
- People — colleagues, her mother, her kids — read her as tired or stressed when she isn't. This
  is the wound. It isn't about beauty, it's about being **misread**.

### 3. Product fatigue and distrust — the reason she doesn't buy

- She has a shelf of half-used serums that promised the same things yours promises. **[E]**
- She cannot tell the difference between a real product and a dropshipped one, and she has been
  burned or knows someone who has. Swedish consumers are unusually alert to this. **[I]**
- She is specifically afraid of retinol: she's heard about peeling, redness and "purging," and
  she has a job where she faces people. She cannot afford two weeks of a bad face. **[E — this
  is the single most-answered objection on your own page, four separate times]**

## 🌟 Goals & Aspirations

**Short-Term Goals** (next 1–3 months)

- To see one visible thing change — usually the pores on her nose — so she can believe the rest
  is coming.
- To get through a workday without the makeup breaking up, and to leave the house some mornings
  without a full base at all.
- To find one product that works and stop researching, stop scrolling, stop buying. The search
  itself is exhausting her.

**Long-Term Aspirations** (1–5 years)

- To age in a way she chose rather than one that happened to her — "look like myself, rested,"
  not "look 30."
- To never need injectables. Not because she judges them (she might), but because she wants to
  stay on the right side of a line she's drawn for herself.
- To feel unremarkable in a photograph. To be in the picture with her kids instead of taking it.

## 🧠 Emotional Drivers & Psychological Insights

- **The gap between inside and outside.** She doesn't feel 47. The mismatch between how she
  feels and how she's read is the actual pain — the pores are just where it shows.
- **She wants relief, not seduction.** Copy written by men in this category defaults to "feel
  sexy again." That's not her driver. Hers is closer to: *not being commented on, not being asked
  if she's tired, not flinching at a photo.* The absence of a negative, not the presence of a
  fantasy.
- **Jantelagen cuts both ways, and this is the Swedish-specific insight.** She wants to look
  better but she does not want to look like she's **tried**. "Gjord" is an insult. Vanity is
  slightly embarrassing to admit out loud. This makes a home routine emotionally *safer* than a
  clinic: nobody sees her go, nobody knows, and the result reads as "hon ser pigg ut" rather
  than "hon har gjort något." **Your product's biggest non-obvious advantage is discretion.**
- **She's a compliance risk, and she knows it.** She has abandoned routines before. Your
  week-by-week roadmap works on her precisely because it tells her what failure will look like
  (the purge) before she experiences it and quits.
- **Price is not the real objection.** 349 kr is less than one dinner out. The objection is
  "another 349 kr wasted," which is a **belief** problem, not a money problem. The guarantee
  addresses the money; only the mechanism addresses the belief.

## 🚩 Key Emotional Fears & Deep Frustrations

- **That she'll be visibly wrecked for two weeks and everyone at work will see.** The purge fear
  is the number one thing standing between her and the "add to cart" button, and it is a *social*
  fear, not a medical one.
- **That she is being taken for a fool again.** Another Meta ad, another 349 kr, another bottle in
  the drawer. Every unsupported claim on your page feeds this fear rather than answering it.
- **That it's simply too late for her** — that pores and lines at 47 are just age, and no cream
  changes age. This is the belief your mechanism has to break, and it's why "de som burit på
  porer och linjer längst ser ofta den tydligaste skillnaden" is the best sentence on your
  current page.

---

## 🧠 Emotional & Psychographic Insights

- **She is solution-aware but mechanism-starved.** She knows what retinol is. She's read the
  articles. What she has never been given is a credible reason why *this* one reaches where the
  others didn't. The 200 000 microneedles is that reason — it should be the loudest thing on the
  page, not the fifth section.
- **She trusts specificity and distrusts enthusiasm.** Swedish consumers are unusually
  hype-resistant. "93% saw results!" reads as advertising. "Vecka 1 kan du få små utslag — det
  är normalt, det går över" reads as honesty, and honesty is what sells here. **Your willingness
  to describe the bad weeks is a conversion asset, not a liability.** Lean harder into it.
- **She will buy the routine, not the bottle** — but only after she believes the routine works.
  This is why the bundle ladder is right and why it should be argued *after* the proof, not
  presented as a discount before it.

## 📌 Typical Emotional Journey

**Awareness.** It's a photo, usually. Someone tags her, or she opens the front camera by accident
in bad light. She sees the pores on her nose and the lines around her mouth and registers, with a
small drop in her stomach, that this is what other people have been seeing. She doesn't act. She
just starts noticing — in shop windows, in Zoom calls, in the car mirror.

**Frustration.** She starts buying. A serum from Lyko, something a colleague recommended, a
sheet mask, an expensive cream at Christmas. She uses each one properly for two or three weeks,
sees nothing, and drifts. The drawer fills up. She starts to suspect the whole category is a
con, and simultaneously that the problem might be *her* — her genes, her age, her skin. The
frustration turns inward, which is why it goes quiet. She stops mentioning it.

**Desperation & Seeking Solutions.** Something forces the issue: a wedding, a milestone birthday,
a divorce, a photo she can't unsee, a friend who suddenly looks great. She googles botox prices
in her town, and either the number or the needle stops her. She looks at clinics. She reads about
prescription retinol and finds the peeling stories. She wants the result those things promise
without the cost, the needles, or the two weeks of a ruined face. **This is the exact moment your
ad reaches her**, and it's why "professionella resultat hemma, skonsamt nog för känslig hud" is
the right core promise — it's the only sentence that resolves all three constraints at once.

**Relief & Commitment.** She buys. Week one, her skin tingles and she nearly stops — this is where
you lose or keep her, and your roadmap is what keeps her. Somewhere around week two or three she
notices her nose in the bathroom mirror and it's *smoother*, and that small, verifiable win
converts her from a buyer into a believer. From there she stops evaluating and starts
maintaining. She tells one or two people, quietly, and only if they ask what she's doing —
because she'd rather they think she just looks well.
